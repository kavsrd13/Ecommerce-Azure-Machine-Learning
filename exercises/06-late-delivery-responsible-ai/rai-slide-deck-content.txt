SLIDE 01 | Title: Can we trust a late-delivery alert?
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Olist e-commerce orders: predict late versus on-time delivery.
- Train on Azure Machine Learning; inspect a Responsible AI dashboard.
- Central question: what would happen if we acted on this model?
SPEAKER NOTES
We are not trying to produce an impressive accuracy number. We want to
learn whether the classifier can support a useful, safe decision. The model
is trained on historical Brazilian e-commerce orders and evaluated on later
orders. A dashboard helps us inspect incorrect predictions and the signals
behind them. It does not certify that the system is fair, accurate, or ready
to send customer notifications.
VISUAL IDEA: Purchase -> promised date -> delivery outcome -> model -> review.
SOURCE/DEMO: lab-guide.html in this lab folder; official RAI guide [R1].

SLIDE 03 | Business scenario: a promised delivery date
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- A customer places an order and sees an estimated delivery date.
- An operations team wants to spot orders at risk of missing that promise.
- Possible action: human review or proactive investigation, not auto-alert.
SPEAKER NOTES
Imagine a seller/customer route with a tight promise and a heavy order.
If a risk signal is reliable, staff might investigate capacity or contact a
carrier. If it produces too many false alerts, it wastes staff time and can
unnecessarily worry customers. Define what action the organization can
actually take before building a classifier; our dataset does not contain
carrier interventions or their costs, so we cannot claim a pickup point or
notification would fix a delay.
VISUAL IDEA: Order placed -> delivery promise -> operations review -> actual date.
SOURCE/DEMO: Olist source dataset [R6].

SLIDE 04 | Why not just report overall accuracy?
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Only 672 of 18,603 future-period orders were late: 3.61%.
- Predicting "on time" for every order yields 96.39% accuracy.
- That trivial rule catches zero late orders.
SPEAKER NOTES
Ask students to calculate the majority-class accuracy: 17,931/18,603.
That classifier looks outstanding by accuracy alone and is useless for the
business question. We need a confusion matrix, late-class precision and
recall, ranking quality, and an explicit discussion of error costs.
Balanced class weighting in our demonstration emphasizes late recall but
does not magically improve precision or calibrate probabilities.
VISUAL IDEA: 96.39% big numeral crossed by "0 late orders found."
SOURCE/DEMO: full Azure ML future-period test from the student's recorded training job.

SLIDE 05 | Dataset: what does one row mean?
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Final learning table: one row per delivered order.
- Orders + items + products + customers + sellers + geolocation.
- Payments and reviews are not needed for this checkout-time question.
SPEAKER NOTES
The public Kaggle listing describes about 100,000 anonymized Olist orders
from 2016-2018. The raw files are related tables, not one flat CSV. Items can repeat
an order ID; geolocation has many coordinate rows for one ZIP prefix.
Summarize items to the order grain and aggregate coordinates by ZIP prefix
before joining. Otherwise a naive merge multiplies observations and
distorts the label rate. We keep a single representative seller, the seller
of the highest-priced item in a multi-seller order. That is an approximation
to discuss, not a perfect description of the shipping route.
VISUAL IDEA: Compact join diagram: items -> orders <- customers; seller and ZIP lookup.
SOURCE/DEMO: prepare.py, order/item joins and ZIP-prefix aggregation.

SLIDE 06 | A precise definition of "late"
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Label: late_delivery = 1 if actual delivery DATE > promised DATE.
- Same-day arrival counts as on time, even after midnight.
- Only delivered orders with a known actual date have this label.
SPEAKER NOTES
The estimated delivery timestamp is recorded as a date boundary. Comparing
raw timestamps would incorrectly mark a package delivered during its
promised day as late. We normalize both timestamps to their calendar dates
before comparing them. Canceled, undelivered, or right-censored orders
have no comparable outcome and are excluded. That exclusion creates
selection bias: the trained population is not every possible new order.
VISUAL IDEA: Promise Oct 10; arrival Oct 10 16:00 -> on time; Oct 11 -> late.
SOURCE/DEMO: prepare.py, label construction.

SLIDE 07 | The decision-time rule prevents leakage
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Use only facts known when the order is placed.
- Inputs: states, promise length, distance, basket count/price/weight, month.
- Never use actual delivery date, post-purchase status, or review score.
SPEAKER NOTES
The actual delivery date is used to construct the historical label, not to
predict a future order. ZIP-prefix Haversine distance is an approximation,
not road travel time. "promised_days" is valid only if the promise is
available at the proposed prediction moment. It is also tightly linked to
the definition of the outcome: changing the promised date can change the
label without changing how fast the parcel moves. This is important when
interpreting its contribution in the RAI dashboard.
VISUAL IDEA: Two columns: "known at checkout" versus "learned afterward."
SOURCE/DEMO: prepare.py FEATURES; src/train.py FEATURES.

SLIDE 08 | Feature engineering is part of the experiment
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Count items; sum item prices and product weights per order.
- Approximate route with customer/seller ZIP-prefix coordinates.
- Calculate straight-line distance; impute missing distance from TRAIN only.
SPEAKER NOTES
Seller and customer states remain categorical inputs. We calculate
promised_days from estimated date minus purchase timestamp, and
purchase_month from purchase timestamp. Four hundred seventy-six orders
in the prepared order-level data lack distance; we fill both splits from
the training median, not from future test values. The script performs
validated joins and asserts uniqueness of order ID. A missing product
weight can become zero after a sum in this simple lab, so a production
system needs more rigorous missingness review.
VISUAL IDEA: Before/after feature table for a single order.
SOURCE/DEMO: prepare.py, FEATURES and distance-median code.

SLIDE 09 | A chronological split, not a random shuffle
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Train: before June 2018; 77,867 orders; 7.53% late.
- Future test: June-August 2018; 18,603 orders; 3.61% late.
- Separate RAI samples: 2,500 train and 3,000 test rows.
SPEAKER NOTES
Grouping at the order level ensures that two items from the same purchase
cannot cross the boundary. The late rate nearly halves across periods:
class prevalence and decision thresholds may not transfer. The test set
reports the model's full-period performance. The stratified RAI samples
bound the cost of explanations and retain class proportions: 188 late
among 2,500 training rows and 108 late among 3,000 dashboard test rows.
Do not mistake a dashboard sample metric for the full 18,603-row result.
VISUAL IDEA: Timeline with train and future-test bands plus two sample icons.
SOURCE/DEMO: prepare.py; outputs from the verified preparation script.

SLIDE 10 | Model: simple enough to audit
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Logistic regression with class_weight="balanced".
- One-hot encode customer and seller states; scale numeric columns.
- Save the entire preprocessing + predictor pipeline as an MLflow model.
SPEAKER NOTES
This is intentionally not a black-box model search. The pipeline accepts
the original tabular feature columns and implements predict and
predict_proba. Balancing the training classes increases attention to rare
late orders, but it can substantially increase false alerts. The pipeline,
not just the LogisticRegression coefficients, is saved so the Azure RAI
components receive the same input schema the model was trained on.
VISUAL IDEA: Raw columns -> OneHot/StandardScaler -> LogisticRegression -> risk.
SOURCE/DEMO: src/train.py; jobs/train.yml.

SLIDE 11 | Interpret the confusion matrix
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- True late predicted late (TP): 521; late missed (FN): 151.
- On time predicted late (FP): 7,860; correctly on time (TN): 10,071.
- 8,381 of 18,603 orders were flagged as late.
SPEAKER NOTES
Ask: which cell is most costly in an operational workflow? A false negative
misses a possible delay; a false positive can waste work or alarm a
customer. The model flags 45.05% of orders, although only 3.61% truly
arrived late. The four counts sum to 18,603; TP + FN equals the 672 actual
late orders. These cloud-run results are observations at the default model
decision rule, not a recommended threshold for use with customers.
VISUAL IDEA: 2x2 confusion matrix with FP and FN highlighted separately.
SOURCE/DEMO: the student's recorded Azure ML run, full-test MLflow metrics.

SLIDE 12 | Precision and recall tell different stories
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Late recall = 521 / 672 = 77.53%.
- Late precision = 521 / 8,381 = 6.22%.
- ROC-AUC = 0.704; average precision = 0.088.
SPEAKER NOTES
Recall asks: of the orders that really were late, how many did we catch?
Precision asks: of all orders we flagged, how many really were late?
Average precision is 8.8%, above the 3.6% future-period prevalence, so
the risk ranking contains signal, but the current decision rule is poor
for automatic alerts. Accuracy at this rule is 56.94%, lower than the
trivial 96.39% majority-class accuracy. These figures are compatible:
class imbalance and error costs make a single metric misleading.
VISUAL IDEA: Two side-by-side fractions; shaded baseline prevalence.
SOURCE/DEMO: src/train.py logged MLflow metrics; full-test job output.

SLIDE 13 | What is Responsible AI in this lesson?
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Ask who and what is affected by model errors.
- Investigate reliability, transparency, and error disparities.
- Treat the Azure dashboard as evidence for review, not certification.
SPEAKER NOTES
Responsible AI requires a defined human purpose and accountability for
mistakes. Azure Machine Learning's Responsible AI dashboard brings
model/data overview, cohort comparisons, error analysis, and explanations
into an inspectable workflow for supported tabular models. It does not
guarantee fairness, safety, privacy, or causal validity. Here the main
stakeholders are customers and operations staff who might act on a risk
flag. A high false-alert count is itself an operational harm to consider.
VISUAL IDEA: Model at center; customer, operations, and audit arrows.
SOURCE/DEMO: Azure dashboard concepts [R2]; viewing [R3]; fairness [R11].

SLIDE 14 | Define the dashboard's questions first
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Reliability: where are false negatives and false positives concentrated?
- Transparency: which features influence this model's risk scores?
- Cohorts and data: do errors or feature distributions change by location?
SPEAKER NOTES
We configure the dashboard to answer specific questions, not simply to
produce attractive charts. An error difference in a geographic cohort
requires sample counts, route context, and further investigation. We do
not have a protected-class attribute or evidence that a state directly
caused an error. Also ask whether the price of a false alert is higher
than the cost of a missed delay before considering changes to a threshold.
VISUAL IDEA: Three questions mapped to overview, error analysis, explanation.
SOURCE/DEMO: jobs/rai-dashboard.yml, error_analysis and explain steps.

SLIDE 15 | Model overview, data, and cohorts
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- View classification outcomes on labeled dashboard data.
- Compare precision, recall, selection rate, and group sample size.
- A small cohort's rate can vary substantially by chance.
SPEAKER NOTES
The dashboard is given 3,000 future-period test rows, not all 18,603.
Only 108 of those rows are late. If a state has very few late orders,
one mistake can cause a large percentage change. Model overview and
cohort filters help find questions to investigate. Data analysis views can
show feature distributions and observed versus predicted outcomes. A
selection rate describes the fraction flagged by the model, not the true
late rate. These views do not justify
declaring one state unfairly treated from a small sample or from an
unadjusted rate. Compare precision, recall, false-positive and
false-negative behavior with counts and operational context.
VISUAL IDEA: Overall metrics card next to two cohort cards with n values.
SOURCE/DEMO: Azure Studio dashboard UI [R3]; data/rai-test/MLTable.

SLIDE 16 | Error analysis: find failure pockets
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- An error tree splits data into segments with different error rates.
- A heat map compares errors across one or two selected features.
- Distinguish a cohort's error rate from its share of all errors.
SPEAKER NOTES
Use the error tree to ask whether certain routes, distances, or customer
states are overrepresented among incorrect predictions. Inspect the
number of rows behind each branch before trusting it. Error RATE means
wrong predictions divided by orders IN the cohort; error COVERAGE means
that cohort's wrong predictions divided by ALL wrong predictions. The
configured heat-map filters are customer_state and distance_km. An error pocket
is not necessarily a causal mechanism: seller practices, carrier delays,
promise policy, and season can all be confounded. The current lab
computes error analysis on a bounded sample and gathers it into the
registered model's dashboard.
VISUAL IDEA: Schematic error tree alongside a state-by-distance heat map.
SOURCE/DEMO: jobs/rai-dashboard.yml error_analysis; RAI [R1]; errors [R10].

SLIDE 17 | Explanations: global versus local
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Global: what inputs does the fitted model generally rely on?
- Local: what influenced one selected prediction?
- Influence is not proof that changing a feature changes delivery speed.
SPEAKER NOTES
For a particular high-risk order, inspect the features used in its score.
For the overall model, check whether promised_days, route distance, and
state are influential in sensible ways. One-hot encoding turns each state
into model-level indicators; the dashboard receives the original state
columns and the full preprocessing pipeline. An explanation describes
the model, not what a carrier would do if a seller moved or a delivery
promise changed. Confirm important features were known at prediction time.
VISUAL IDEA: One global contribution chart and one single-order explanation.
SOURCE/DEMO: jobs/rai-dashboard.yml explain; model explainability [R1].

SLIDE 18 | Tools we intentionally did not run
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- No counterfactual: "change customer state" is not an action.
- No causal analysis: observational Olist data lacks a defined intervention.
- No fairness certification: geographic cohorts are not protected traits.
SPEAKER NOTES
Azure provides optional counterfactual and causal components for supported
scenarios, but being able to execute a component is not evidence that its
result is meaningful. Changing promised_days may mechanically change what
"late" means; it is not the same as accelerating transport. A causal
claim would require an intervention, assumptions about confounders, and
appropriate study design. Restrict this introductory dashboard to error
analysis and explanations, which we can interpret responsibly. Microsoft
labels the separate no-code Studio generation experience public preview;
our exercise uses the CLI v2 pipeline, not that no-code creation flow.
VISUAL IDEA: Two optional branches off the pipeline, labeled "requires design."

SLIDE 19 | Azure ML architecture at a glance
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Kaggle CSVs -> local preparation -> four Parquet-backed MLTables.
- Azure ML cloud training -> MLflow sklearn model -> registered model v1.
- RAI constructor -> error analysis + explanation -> gather -> Studio.
SPEAKER NOTES
The four data assets are full train, full test, sampled RAI train, and
sampled RAI test. The cloud training job uses the full train and full
future test; the RAI pipeline uses the smaller sampled datasets. The
registered model is a complete scikit-learn pipeline, not a bare pickle.
The RAI constructor loads that model and data; two analysis components
run; gather assembles a dashboard associated with the registered model.
Everything is versioned in Azure ML to aid reproducibility.

SLIDE 26 | What we can responsibly conclude today
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Ranking has some signal; the default decision rule is not acceptable.
- Recall 77.5%, but precision 6.2% and 7,860 false alerts.
- Do not automate customer-facing warnings from this run.
SPEAKER NOTES
For this historical sample the model detects many delays, but the false
alert burden is far too large for unreviewed notifications. The result
could motivate a lower-volume, human-reviewed investigation workflow;
that decision requires a separate business cost analysis, calibration,
and prospective evaluation. Do not claim the dashboard itself improved
the model: it made risks visible. A deployment decision needs explicit
threshold, acceptable error budgets and governance approval.

SLIDE 27 | Important limitations and safer next experiment
ON-SLIDE TEXT
- Delivered-only training omits canceled and still-undelivered orders.
- 2016-2018 data and a changing late rate limit current relevance.
- Create train/validation/test time windows before threshold tuning.
SPEAKER NOTES
The RAI test sample has only 108 late examples, so small-state slices
are uncertain. Geographic distance is ZIP-prefix straight-line distance
and the multi-seller route is simplified. If promised_days was not known
when a proposed alert would be issued, the model needs to be rebuilt.
The current demonstration logs future-test metrics but has no separate
validation period for threshold selection. To improve it, define an
earlier validation window, choose a threshold against measured alert
capacity, and reserve a later untouched test period for one final check.
VISUAL IDEA: Risk register with data, model, and deployment columns.

PRESENTER'S FINAL CAUTION
The dashboard exposes model behavior and observed errors; it does not
guarantee that this model is responsible, fair, causal, current, or fit
for customer-facing decisions. An actual launch needs newer data,
threshold/cost analysis, representative cohorts, human accountability,
and prospective monitoring.
